# 7 Tips for a Great Testimonial Video (From a Producer Who Films Them Every Week)

The difference between a testimonial video that makes donors reach for their wallets and one that gets politely watched and forgotten comes down to a handful of production decisions — most of which happen before you ever press record.

Here's what works, based on producing testimonial videos for nonprofits, churches, and businesses in Tennessee.

---

## 1. Prepare Your Subject Before the Day of the Shoot

The most common mistake in testimonial production is sending a subject into an interview with no preparation. Nervousness kills authenticity. Send a prep guide at least 3 days before the shoot that covers:

- What to wear (solid colors; avoid logos, stripes, or white)
- What questions will be asked (not a script — just the themes)
- How to answer in complete sentences, not "yes/no" fragments
- What to expect on set (a relaxed conversation, not a performance)

Subjects who arrive prepared give usable material in 30–45 minutes. Unprepared subjects can take 90 minutes and still deliver shaky results.

---

## 2. Ask for the Specific Moment, Not the General Feeling

The best testimonial answers come from specific memories, not general reflections.

**Weak question:** "How has this organization impacted your life?"

**Better question:** "Can you describe a specific moment — a day, a conversation, a decision — where you noticed something had changed?"

Specific moments produce specific answers. Specific answers produce emotional responses in viewers. General answers sound like press releases.

---

## 3. Don't Film in a Conference Room If You Can Avoid It

Location sends a signal. A fluorescent-lit conference room signals "this was obligatory." A meaningful location — the program space where the work happens, the outdoor setting where the subject found clarity, the workspace where the team operates — signals "this is real."

When choosing a location, ask: where does this person's story actually live? Film there.

If you have no other option than a conference room, bring lighting. Practical ambient light from a window plus a simple LED key light makes a significant difference.

---

## 4. Give Subjects Permission to Pause

Subjects who feel pressure to keep talking without stopping produce rambling, unfocused answers. At the start of the interview, say this explicitly: "If you lose your train of thought or want to start an answer over, just pause. We can always cut and try again. There are no mistakes."

This one instruction reduces editing time substantially. Subjects relax. Answers improve. The edit becomes a process of selecting the best version of what they said, not rescuing the only usable take.

---

## 5. Film More B-Roll Than You Think You Need

B-roll — the footage that plays over the interview audio — is what transforms a talking-head video into a documentary-style piece. It provides visual context, breaks up the interview footage, and allows the editor to cut around stumbles, restarts, and pauses without a visible jump cut.

Rule of thumb: for every 1 minute of interview audio you plan to use, you need 3–4 minutes of b-roll to cover it. Film your subject doing their work. Film the environment. Film the details that tell the larger story.

---

## 6. Capture More Than One Emotional Register

A testimonial that goes from difficult → hopeful → resolved works because it moves. If every answer carries the same emotional weight — all gratitude, all positivity — the video becomes flat. Ask at least one question that invites the subject to describe what things were like before — the difficulty, the frustration, the uncertainty. That contrast is what makes the resolution land.

If your organization serves vulnerable populations or trauma survivors, be thoughtful about how deeply you go. The goal is resonance, not retraumatization.

---

## 7. Edit to the Point

The finished hero cut should be 2–4 minutes. Not 7. Not 10. Two to four.

Nonprofit organizations sometimes want to include everything — every insight, every detail, every thank-you. Resist this. A 2-minute testimonial that's tight and emotionally complete will be watched, rewatched, and shared. A 9-minute testimonial will be started and abandoned.

The job of the edit is to find the narrative spine of what the subject said and build the shortest version that communicates it fully. Everything else is cut.

---

## What to Avoid

- **Reading from a script on camera.** It always reads as scripted. Prepare subjects with themes and let them speak naturally.
- **Filming in bad audio environments.** Traffic, HVAC noise, and echo destroy an otherwise good interview. Scout your location in advance and listen.
- **Rushing the interview.** Give the subject time to settle in. The first 10 minutes of an interview are almost never the best material.
- **Skipping music licensing.** Unlicensed music gets your video muted or removed from social platforms. Budget for it.

---

## How Long Should a Testimonial Video Be?

- **Hero cut (website, YouTube, presentations):** 2–4 minutes
- **Social cut (Instagram, Facebook, LinkedIn):** 60–90 seconds
- **Short clip (Reels, Shorts, ads):** 15–30 seconds

Produce all three from the same shoot. The footage is there — it costs a fraction of the original shoot to cut additional formats.
